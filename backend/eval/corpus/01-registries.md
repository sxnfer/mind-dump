# How carbon credit registries track credits

A carbon credit registry is a database that records every credit from the moment it is issued until it is used. Each credit carries a unique serial number that encodes the project, the vintage year and a position in the issuance batch. Because the serial number is unique, two buyers cannot both claim the same tonne: the registry would show the conflict.

Issuance happens only after a verifier has checked the project's monitoring report. The registry then creates a block of credits, one per tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent, and places them in the project developer's account. From there they can be transferred between accounts many times, and every transfer is written to the registry's ledger with a timestamp.

Retirement is the final step. When a company retires a credit, the registry moves it to a retirement account and marks it as permanently used, together with the name of the beneficiary and the reason. A retired credit can never be transferred again. Public retirement records are what let outsiders check a company's offset claims.

Double counting is the problem registries exist to prevent. It comes in three forms: double issuance, when two registries issue credits for the same reduction; double use, when one credit backs two claims; and double claiming, when a host country counts the same reduction toward its own national target. Registries compare their records with each other to catch the first form.

Registries also hold the project documents: the project description, each validation and verification report, and the monitoring reports. Anyone can download them, which lets journalists and researchers check a project's claims against its own paperwork years after the credits were sold.

Account holders must pass identity checks before they can open an account, a step registries call know your customer. The checks exist because credits are valuable and easy to move, which once made registries a target for fraud and money laundering.
