# Permanence and buffer pools

Permanence is the question of how long stored carbon stays out of the atmosphere. A tonne avoided at a power plant is gone for good, but a tonne stored in a tree can return to the air if the forest burns, is cut or dies from drought. Credits from storage projects therefore carry a reversal risk that credits from avoided emissions do not.

Buffer pools are the main defence. Every forestry or soil project deposits a share of its credits, typically between ten and twenty percent, into a shared buffer account that no one can sell. The share depends on a risk assessment covering fire, pests, political instability and the developer's finances.

When a reversal happens, the registry cancels credits from the buffer pool equal to the carbon lost. If the loss was unintentional, such as a wildfire, the project owes nothing more. If it was intentional, such as logging the protected area, the developer must replace the lost credits from its own account, and the project may be suspended.

Severe wildfire seasons have shown that some buffer pools can be drawn down far faster than their designers assumed. A pool sized for rare, independent events struggles when one hot, dry summer burns many projects at once, because the losses arrive together instead of spreading over decades.

Monitoring for reversals continues after the crediting period ends. Many forestry standards require projects to keep reporting on their carbon stock for decades, and a project that stops reporting may have its buffer contribution cancelled as if the carbon had been lost.

Engineered removals, such as carbon mineralised into rock, face a much smaller reversal risk than forests. Buyers often pay more for them for that reason, even though each tonne costs far more to produce.
