# Additionality: would it have happened anyway?

A project is additional when the emission reductions would not have happened without the money from selling carbon credits. If a wind farm was already profitable on electricity sales alone, its credits do not represent any extra climate benefit: the turbines would have been built regardless. Additionality is the test that separates real impact from paperwork.

The investment analysis is the most common test. The developer shows that the project's internal rate of return without credit revenue falls below a benchmark, such as the typical return investors demand in that country and sector, and that credit revenue lifts it above. Auditors check the spreadsheet, the inputs and whether the benchmark is reasonable.

A barrier analysis is the alternative when the obstacle is not money. The developer shows that something other than cost blocked the project, for example no local technicians, no access to loans, or a technology no one in the region had used before, and that carbon finance helps to overcome it.

Common practice analysis is the final check. If a technology is already widely used in the region (a methodology might set the threshold at twenty percent market share), it is assumed to be common practice and therefore not additional, whatever the other tests say. Critics note that all three tests rely on claims about a world that never happened, which no one can observe.

Regulatory surplus is a further condition many standards add. If a law already requires the activity, such as capturing methane at landfills above a certain size, the reductions are not additional, because the operator had to make them anyway.

Some standards skip project-by-project tests for activities that are rare everywhere, such as small solar systems in the least developed countries. These positive lists treat a whole category as additional, which cuts paperwork but trusts that the list is kept up to date.
